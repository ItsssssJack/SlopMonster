# Worked example — Ridgeline Roofing

A full site build. It arrived as a wireframe of `Lorem ipsum` and "Medium length section
heading goes here". Every shipped line went through this system. This file is the record.

## Six tells, caught and cut

Every one of these showed up in a first draft of the page and was rewritten on sight.

**The three-item flourish**
> ~~Trusted, reliable and built to last.~~
> **Six nails per shingle, every shingle.**
> Three adjectives is a rhythm, not an argument. One specification beats it every time.

**The `not just X, but Y` shape**
> ~~Not just a roof, but peace of mind.~~
> **A written scope and a fixed number before anyone climbs a ladder.**
> The construction promises a reveal and then delivers an abstraction.

**Puffed-up verbs**
> ~~We leverage industry-leading materials to deliver unparalleled protection.~~
> **We source materials from manufacturers who test for wind, hail and sun.**
> `leverage`, `deliver`, `unparalleled`, `seamless`, `robust`, `elevate`. All of them mean nothing and cost a line.

**Empty superlatives**
> ~~The area's most trusted roofing experts.~~
> **Roofing, and only roofing, since 2001.**
> "Most trusted" is unfalsifiable, so the reader discounts it entirely. A date cannot be argued with.

**Em-dash pile-up**
> ~~Our team — trained, certified and local — is ready to help.~~
> **Thirty-eight on the crew, factory-trained for every material we install.**
> One dash in a paragraph is punctuation. Three is a tic.

**Hollow proof**
> ~~Loved by 10,000+ happy homeowners.~~
> **Project names and photography are placeholders — swap in your own jobs before this goes live.**
> Inventing a number is the one unrecoverable mistake. Say the slot is empty instead.

## Where the shipped lines came from

Four sources, and if a sentence cannot name its source it does not go on the page:

1. **What the trade actually does** — the strongest source. "Six nails per shingle, every
   shingle" is a real specification with a real failure mode behind it. Nothing invented
   sounds like that, because invented copy does not know it.
2. **What the customer already fears** — that the number will move, that the yard will be
   wrecked, that they are being sold a whole roof for a flashing problem. The page
   answers those three, in order.
3. **What the competition will not say** — refusals travel further than promises. "We do
   not do overlays" and "we do not quote from the driveway" both position and disqualify
   in one line.
4. **The wireframe's own surviving lines** — "From first call to final nail" arrived
   already written and was better than anything a rewrite would produce. It stayed.

## The mechanical cleanse checklist used on the finished page

1. **Strip the adjectives.** Delete every adjective, read it back, restore only the ones
   whose absence changed the meaning. Usually one in five survives.
2. **Find the number.** Any claim that could carry a number gets one, or gets cut.
   "Long-lasting" becomes "25 years". "Fast" becomes "4.2 hours".
3. **Name the noun.** "Materials" becomes "ice and water shield". "Quality workmanship"
   becomes "six nails per shingle". The specific noun is the whole trick.
4. **Cut the throat-clearing.** Delete the first sentence of each paragraph and check
   whether anything was lost. Usually it was a run-up.
5. **Read it aloud.** Anything you would not say standing on a driveway comes out.
6. **Verify every claim.** Numbers, certifications and warranty terms get checked against
   reality before publishing. Anything unverified is marked as a placeholder *on the
   page*, not quietly left in.

Point 6 is not decorative. The Ridgeline build carried five unverified wireframe figures
(25+ years, 4,800 roofs, 38 technicians, 50-year warranty, 4.2-hour call-out) and the
page says so, in a visible callout, rather than shipping them as fact.
